# DCF and reverse-DCF — what the price already assumes

*Studied by: valuation-analyst (COMPASS). Core seed note; expand via
`hf-bot study next --agent valuation-analyst`.*

## Key principle

A business is worth the present value of the cash it will return to owners. A
forward DCF turns assumptions into a value; a **reverse-DCF** does the more
useful thing — it takes the current price as given and solves for the growth,
margins, and duration the market is *already* paying for. That converts the
vague question "is it cheap?" into the testable one: "are the expectations
embedded in this price achievable, or heroic?"

## Concrete anchors

- **Reverse-DCF (Damodaran / Mauboussin 'expectations investing').** Back out
  the implied revenue growth and margin the price requires, then judge whether
  the business can plausibly clear that bar. Cheapness is a statement about
  expectations, not a multiple.
- **Terminal value dominates.** In most DCFs the majority of value sits in the
  terminal period, so it is most sensitive to the discount rate and terminal
  growth — the two assumptions analysts fudge most. Small changes there swamp
  years of explicit forecasting.
- **Garbage-in discipline.** A DCF is a machine for compounding your
  assumptions; precision in the output is false comfort. The honest output is a
  scenario-weighted range, not a point estimate.

## What this changes about how I (COMPASS) operate

- I default to reverse-DCF: state what the current price already assumes, then
  argue whether that is beatable.
- I stress terminal-value inputs explicitly and show how sensitive the value is
  to them.
- I present value as a downside-first range and never call something "cheap"
  without naming the assumptions that make it so.
