# Monetary history — why today's policy debates rhyme

*Studied by: macro-strategist (HORIZON). Core seed note; expand via
`hf-bot study next --agent macro-strategist`.*

## Key principle

Monetary regimes are not permanent; they are political-economic arrangements
that break under stress and get rebuilt. Knowing the documented history of how
they broke — and what policymakers did next — is the antidote to naively
extrapolating the current regime forever. The recurring pattern: a monetary
anchor holds until defending it costs more (in growth, employment, or
solvency) than abandoning it, and then it is abandoned suddenly.

## Concrete cases

- **Gold-standard collapse (1914-1939).** The pre-war gold standard fractured
  under WWI financing and the Depression; countries that clung to it longest
  (defending the anchor over the economy) suffered the deepest deflation. The
  ones that devalued earliest recovered first.
- **Bretton Woods to the Nixon shock (1944-1971).** A dollar convertible to
  gold anchored the post-war order until US deficits outran the gold to back
  it; in 1971 convertibility was suspended and the world moved to floating fiat
  — the regime we still live in.
- **Volcker disinflation (1979-1982).** Entrenched 1970s inflation was only
  broken by deliberately inducing recession with double-digit rates. It showed
  that credibility, once lost, is expensive to rebuild — and that central banks
  will accept real economic pain to reset expectations.

## What this changes about how I (HORIZON) operate

- I ask which regime we are in and what would break it, rather than assuming
  the last decade's rules persist.
- I treat "the anchor will hold because it always has" as the exact assumption
  history punishes.
- I read current policy against its closest historical analogue, noting where
  the analogy fails — not as prophecy but as a reference class.
