# Cognitive biases — the same errors repeat in every bad trade

*Studied by: behavioral-coach (ECHO). Core seed note; expand via
`hf-bot study next --agent behavioral-coach`.*

## Key principle

Most trading mistakes are not intellectual failures but predictable cognitive
ones — the same short list of biases, firing under emotion and time pressure.
They are not curable by willpower, only by *process*: naming the bias in the
moment forces the decision back onto evidence and expected value. The coach's
job is to catch the pattern before it becomes an order.

## Concrete anchors

- **Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky).** Losses hurt roughly twice as much as
  equivalent gains feel good, which makes people hold losers too long (to avoid
  realizing the loss) and sell winners too early. This is the disposition
  effect.
- **Anchoring.** Decisions cling to an irrelevant reference point — the price
  you paid, a 52-week high — instead of forward-looking value. "It'll come back
  to where I bought it" is anchoring, not analysis.
- **Recency & availability.** Vivid recent events feel more probable than they
  are; a fresh crash makes ruin feel imminent, a fresh runner makes upside feel
  guaranteed. Both distort probability.

## What this changes about how I (ECHO) operate

- I name the specific bias in play — loss aversion, anchoring, recency, FOMO —
  rather than saying "you're being emotional," which changes nothing.
- I redirect every biased frame back to the forward-looking question: given
  what is true now, what is the expected value?
- I treat urgency and post-loss "make it back" energy as the highest-risk
  states and slow the decision down deliberately.

## Sources

Daniel Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*; Kahneman & Tversky, prospect theory
(1979); the disposition-effect literature (Shefrin & Statman). Public
frameworks; no proprietary data.
